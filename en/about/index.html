01

September 2026 · BlueDot Impact · AGI Strategy course completed

Extending occupational health to the challenges of AI safety.

Course completed
AGI
Strategy
BlueDot Impact · 2026
A stronger conviction
“Occupational health is now also an AI safety issue.”

Preventing the effects of artificial intelligence requires examining individual experience, the organisation of work and the systemic risks that could affect entire populations.

I completed BlueDot Impact’s AGI Strategy course to complement my work on AI-induced psychosocial risks, which had previously focused mainly on effects at the individual worker level.

Broadening the frame without losing sight of real work

Throughout the course, we collectively explored the different pathways through which advanced AI systems could cause harm, the potential scale of their consequences, the fragility of current safeguards and the time available to strengthen them.

Rapid advances in model capabilities and the rise of agents able to coordinate increasingly complex tasks mean that analysis can no longer be confined to the individual workplace, or even to a single organisation. Emerging signals concerning employment, particularly among early-career workers, reinforce the need for vigilance.

This reflection led me to consider these transformations as a potential major public health issue. Worldwide, hundreds of millions of workers could gradually face greater work intensity, increased emotional demands, reduced autonomy, deteriorating workplace relationships, value conflicts or greater employment insecurity.

01 / ScaleFrom the workplace to public health.

The potential effects extend beyond protecting one worker or governing one organisation.

02 / PreventionAct before risks spread.

Prolonged exposure to these factors may cause lasting harm to mental and physical health.

03 / DirectionBuild an approach to AI safety grounded in work.

Connect benchmarks, evaluations, red-teaming exercises and governance frameworks to workplace realities.

Work already under way

This website, seminars, practical resources and my role as AI lead for the French Society of Occupational Health are already helping to structure this work and make it accessible to practitioners.

Next step

Following the course, I decided to expand my work towards occupational-health-focused AI safety benchmarks, evaluations and red-teaming exercises, as well as fit-for-purpose governance frameworks.

02

16 January 2026 · ISTNF · Nearly 400 participants

The first Marcel Marchand Day dedicated to artificial intelligence.

On 16 January 2026, nearly 400 occupational physicians, residents and occupational health nurses attended the first Marcel Marchand Day dedicated to artificial intelligence. I had the honour of co-organising the event with ISTNF, at the initiative of Paul Frimat.

Supporting a major transformation of work

Artificial intelligence is gradually becoming part of the world of work and is expected to profoundly alter its balance in the years ahead. Recent months have already shown that it can have tangible and sometimes troubling consequences for workers: redundancies, rising unemployment in some sectors, changes to the very nature of occupations and transformations in working conditions.

This technological revolution also represents a major opportunity. Used appropriately, AI can become a genuine lever for improvement for employers and workers alike, for example by taking on certain repetitive tasks and allowing people to focus on higher-value activities.

Yet this potential can only be fully realised if these changes are properly supported. Occupational health professionals will have an essential role to play. We need to develop our expertise now so that we can anticipate, assess and prevent the emerging risks associated with these new uses as effectively as possible.

The afternoon programme

An initial opportunity to reflect and share perspectives across five themes.

  1. 01

    A general introduction to artificial intelligence and language models, presented by Dr Ramy Azzouz, hospital practitioner at Lille University Hospital and coordinator of the university diploma in AI and health.

  2. 02

    Lessons from practical applications of LLMs in occupational health through the example of RAVAIL – Travail Santé, presented by Dr Quentin Nocture and Pierre Flamant.

  3. 03

    An analysis of the psychosocial issues associated with the growth of LLMs and their implications for our discipline, which I had the opportunity to present.

  4. 04

    A presentation on using AI to detect and prevent musculoskeletal disorders, given by Axelle Savadogo, an occupational medicine resident.

  5. 05

    A broader reflection on the human dimension of this technological revolution, led by Charles Gorintin, co-founder of Alan as well as non-executive co-founder and board member of Mistral AI.

What comes next

I hope this first event met participants’ expectations and opened up new avenues for reflection. Further events on this topic are very likely to follow. In the meantime, I remain available to participants who would like to continue the discussion.

Acknowledgements

My warm thanks go to all participants and speakers, and to Stéphane Vandenbussche and Mustapha GHAMMADI for their involvement in organising the event.

03

21 October 2025 · French Ministry for the Economy and Finance · DGAFP

AI, work and mental health in the French civil service.

At the invitation of the DGAFP — the French Directorate-General for Administration and the Civil Service — I spoke at the Ministry for the Economy, Finance and Industrial and Digital Sovereignty as part of the conference “Work and mental health: what challenges for the civil service?”, organised for the European Week for Safety and Health at Work.

A collective reflection

During a panel discussion with Valérie Bouvier and Fanny Pastant, moderated by Fabien Piazzon, I addressed the consequences of using artificial intelligence in the French civil service.

Key points

The conditions required for an automation project to provide genuine support for work.

  1. 01

    A growing number of civil servants will see at least some of their tasks automated by artificial intelligence in the years ahead.

  2. 02

    This automation can disrupt how work is organised and may even profoundly transform its nature.

  3. 03

    These changes can contribute to a loss of meaning or autonomy, value conflicts, cognitive overload, hypervigilance or job insecurity: all well-established psychosocial risk factors.

  4. 04

    For now, artificial intelligence remains a tool. To become genuinely enabling, it must be adapted to people, not the other way round.

  5. 05

    Automation projects must be co-designed with managers, staff, social partners and occupational prevention and health services.

  6. 06

    Training and skills development are essential if staff are to understand and take ownership of these new tools.

  7. 07

    When a project is well designed and automation reflects the needs of the service and its staff, it can become a source of satisfaction and recognition at work. In particular, it can free up time for activities with greater human added value: creativity, decision-making, team cohesion and contact with the public.

  8. 08

    Given the challenges ahead for mental health and work retention, occupational prevention and health professionals must engage with these issues quickly so that they can advise and support managers and staff as effectively as possible.

Acknowledgements

I thank the DGAFP for inviting me to contribute to this discussion, and Valérie Bouvier, Fanny Pastant and Fabien Piazzon for the quality of our exchange.
