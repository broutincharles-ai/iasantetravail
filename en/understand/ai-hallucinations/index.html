AI hallucinations: why models get things wrong

A generative AI can confidently present a reference, a figure or a legal article that does not exist. This page explains where these errors, which the guide calls confabulations, come from and where to place the checks.

By Dr Charles Broutin, occupational physician · Updated on 13 November 2026

Read the guide chapter →Guide contents →

The essentials

A plausible answer is not a true answer.

What the word refers to, and why the error cannot be fixed with a setting.

Direct answer

A hallucination, or confabulation, is a plausible but false answer: an invented reference, legal article or figure, presented with confidence. The model produces what is probable in the context, not what is true.

These limits are not simple tuning defects. They follow from the way models are trained, evaluated and built into tools.

Kalai et al., OpenAI, 2025 ↗ · Guide, chapter 05

01 / Six errors

Six errors that persist.

Knowing them helps place the checks in the right spot.

ErrorWhere it comes fromAt work
ConfabulationPre-training does not teach the model to tell a rare fact from a plausible invention, and evaluations that score only accuracy reward guessing over abstaining. Kalai et al., 2025 ↗Invented references, legal articles or figures, presented with confidence.
SycophancyTuning on human preferences can favour approval at the expense of accuracy. In April 2025, OpenAI withdrew a GPT-4o update judged excessively flattering. OpenAI, 2025 ↗The tool tends to confirm the hypothesis contained in the question asked.
Jagged frontierCapabilities do not follow the difficulty a human perceives: excellent here, failing right next door. Dell’Acqua et al., 2023 ↗Trust earned on one task does not transfer to the neighbouring task.
Displayed reasoningThe reasoning a model displays does not faithfully reflect what determined its answer: in an Anthropic study, the models tested reported a hint they had used in only 25 to 39% of cases. Chen et al., 2025 ↗A convincing justification does not prove that the answer is well founded.
Prompt injectionThe model does not reliably separate the user’s instructions from the text it reads: a web page, an email or a PDF can contain hidden instructions. OWASP, LLM01:2025 ↗The risk rises as soon as the tool can send, modify or delete.
VariabilityGeneration is probabilistic and sensitive to wording, to the order of documents and to the model version. Its knowledge stops at a cut-off date, unless it can consult live sources.Test several times, date the trials and record the version used.

02 / Two studies

What two studies show.

One on consultants, the other in occupational health.

758 consultants

Excellent here, failing right next door.

In the trial by Dell’Acqua and colleagues (Harvard Business School, BCG), AI improved speed and quality on tasks within its reach: 12% more tasks completed, 25% faster. On a task just beyond it, consultants helped by AI gave the right answer less often (−19 points).

Dell’Acqua et al., 2023 ↗
385 recommendations

No hallucination observed, with no general guarantee.

In the study published by the INRS in June 2026, the o1 model reviewed medical recommendations stripped of identifying details: no hallucination was detected in that setting. The result holds for one model, one prompt and one specific corpus; it does not guarantee their absence in other uses.

03 / At work

Where to place the checks.

Five habits, whatever the tool.

  • Check what can be checked. References, figures and legal articles can be invented.
  • Be wary of an answer that confirms your hypothesis too quickly, and of instructions hidden in a document the tool reads.
  • Do not use as it stands a result you cannot verify. Ask for a competent review or keep the task out of the tool: producing a result with AI does not mean knowing how to judge its quality.
  • Name who checks. A competent person, with the review built into their workload and the sources to compare the answer against.
  • Date the trials. Test several times and record the model version.

Pointers for employees · Five questions before professional use

Key answers

Three frequent questions.

Short answers, drawn from the guide.

Can hallucinations be eliminated?

The guide presents no way of doing so: these errors follow from the way models are trained and evaluated. Supplying documents anchors the answers in your sources, but you still have to check the extracts actually retrieved and cited.

Is a model that explains its reasoning more reliable?

Not for that reason. The reasoning it displays does not faithfully reflect what determined its answer, and a convincing explanation can accompany a false answer.

Why does AI so often agree with me?

Because tuning on human preferences can favour approval at the expense of accuracy: this is sycophancy. The tool then tends to confirm the hypothesis contained in the question asked.
