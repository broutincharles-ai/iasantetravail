Introduction

Broaden what we mean by “safety”.

The debate on AI safety is still largely focused on model performance and model-level risks — errors, bias, cybersecurity and misuse — while paying far less attention to the risks created when AI is deployed at work. Yet AI can reshape work organisation, autonomy, cognitive load, surveillance, job security and skill retention: dimensions with well-established consequences for mental and physical health. With nearly one in four workers worldwide potentially exposed to generative AI, hundreds of millions of people may be affected. AI safety must therefore extend beyond model safety and become a shared concern for AI laboratories and researchers, governments, employers, and occupational safety and health professionals, so these effects can be assessed and prevented by design and before deployment.

01 / Define the scope

The safety of an AI system cannot be reduced to its technical performance.

AI system safety means preventing the harm these systems may cause. It includes response reliability, cybersecurity, bias, misuse and our ability to retain meaningful control over the system.

When an AI tool is introduced at work, the analysis must also cover the organisation: which tasks change, who checks the output, who decides, who is accountable for errors, and under what conditions a worker can report a problem or refuse to use the tool.

The criterion to retain

When a foreseeable AI-related change in tasks or work organisation exposes workers to an avoidable risk to their physical or mental health, that risk is also a deployment-safety issue.

01

Check how the tool performs

Response reliability, stability, cybersecurity, bias, traceability and risks of misuse.

02

Examine working conditions

Workload and pace, room for manoeuvre, responsibilities, job security, skill retention and cooperation.

03

Involve occupational health upstream

Bring prevention professionals in before the tool is selected, then monitor deployment effects with the workers concerned.

02 / From the model to real work

How introducing an AI tool can create occupational risk.

The tool never acts alone. Its effects depend on the task assigned to it, the authority given to its output, and organisational choices about time, staffing and control. These changes can affect all six families of psychosocial risk factors described in the Gollac framework.

01 · Define the use

Specify the task and the tool’s role

Does the AI assist, rank cases, monitor activity or directly contribute to a decision? An error does not have the same consequences in each situation.

02 · Organise oversight

Identify the additional work

Who reviews the output? With what information and within what timeframe? Who corrects errors, handles unusual cases and responds to challenges?

03 · Decide how gains are used

Determine what happens to saved time

It may reduce workload, improve quality or free time for training. It may also lead to higher targets, shorter deadlines or job cuts.

04 · Monitor the effects

Compare conditions before and after

Monitor workload, room for manoeuvre, errors, skills, mutual support, absenteeism and reported difficulties.

Having a human in the loop does not, by itself, guarantee meaningful human control.

The six psychosocial risk factors applied to AI.

Introducing AI does not automatically create psychosocial risk. It can, however, worsen any of these six dimensions when it changes workload, decision rules, working relationships or job prospects without adequate prevention.

Gollac framework factorExample of an AI-related work situation
01Work intensity and working timeProduction becomes faster, but targets rise and output review is added without extra time or staffing.
02Emotional demandsAI handles simple requests, leaving workers with the most conflictual cases or situations involving distress.
03Lack of autonomyThe system’s recommendation becomes a de facto instruction; departing from it must be justified or may be penalised.
04Deteriorated social relations at workUsing an assistant replaces some exchanges between colleagues, while automated monitoring or individual rankings create mistrust.
05Conflicts of valuesA worker must quickly approve an answer they consider incomplete, inconsistent with professional standards or insufficient for quality work.
06Job insecurityAutomation is announced without clarifying which tasks, jobs, training opportunities or redeployment options will be affected.

05 / Prevention case

Before deployment, specify how risks to workers will be prevented.

This record describes the measures planned before launch, accountable people, stopping criteria and post-deployment monitoring arrangements.

01

Identify risks before purchase

Describe the real task, existing workload, workers affected, possible errors and expected additional work.

02

Involve workers and their representatives

Discuss task organisation, targets, training, means of redress and reasons for suspending the system.

03

Plan for additional workload

Include review, correction, learning and unusual-case handling in deadlines and staffing levels.

04

Provide a real right to raise concerns and stop

Allow workers to challenge an output or interrupt use without penalty.

05

Preserve skills and cooperation

Maintain independent practice, training for newcomers and exchanges between colleagues.

06

Verify effects and correct course

Monitor workload, errors, absenteeism, skills and reported difficulties; modify or withdraw the system where necessary.
