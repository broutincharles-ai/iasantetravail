The way AI transforms work can affect our health and our economic and social power. Occupational health has the means to identify these changes and take preventive action early.

Dr Charles Broutin, occupational physician · AI lead for the French Society of Occupational Health (SFST)

AI Safety · Systemic risks

The AI / prevention ratio

At scale, AI-driven changes in work can contribute to systemic risks. Psychosocial risks can become a public health issue. The replacement of tasks and skills can also weaken workers’ economic and social power.

The framework / AI Safety

What is AI Safety?

Here, AI Safety means the research and practices aimed at preventing and reducing the harm that artificial intelligence systems can cause to people and society.

It concerns their design, use, evaluation and governance. Their effects also depend on the people, organisations and conditions involved in developing and deploying them: this is a sociotechnical approach.

For general-purpose AI, the International AI Safety Report 2026 distinguishes three broad categories of risk:

Malicious use

Using AI to deceive, manipulate or facilitate dangerous acts.

Malfunctions

Errors, unexpected behaviour and difficulties maintaining control of a system.

Systemic risks

Effects of widespread adoption, including on employment and human autonomy.

Taking action means identifying risks before deployment, evaluating systems under their actual conditions of use, monitoring incidents and being able to correct or discontinue a use.

This site's thesis: occupational health can contribute by observing changes in work, their effects on health and people's capacity to act. It can also examine the conditions in which teams develop and evaluate AI. This contribution still needs to be documented and evaluated; it does not, on its own, encompass AI Safety.

The starting point

Social change is already taking shape at work.

Automating a task can redistribute work rather than eliminate it. Who produces? Who checks? Who decides? Who retains the skills and benefits from the gains? This is where the analysis begins.

What AI adds

Checking outputs, correcting errors, handling exceptions and taking on new responsibilities.

What AI removes

Certain tasks, but also, depending on its use, opportunities to learn, make decisions or work with others.

What AI redistributes

Time, staffing, the value produced, responsibilities and the ability to influence decisions.

A change of scale

Two collective risks to examine together.

What happens within a team can become a societal issue when the same changes spread and persist. Job losses are not the only signal to monitor.

Disempowerment

The loss of economic and social power.

If AI progressively replaces human work and capabilities, people's contribution to production may become less indispensable. Their bargaining power, economic security and collective influence could then weaken.

01 Progressive replacement of human tasks and capabilities02 Greater dependence and weaker bargaining power03 Possible reduction in economic and social influence

The issue extends beyond autonomy in one's job: it concerns the place of individuals and groups in the decisions that shape their lives.

A hypothetical trajectory. This scenario is neither inevitable nor established. Signals observed at work are not validated predictors of a societal shift.

Public health

From psychosocial risks to health effects.

Intensification, insecurity, loss of autonomy, isolation or value conflicts: adverse changes in work can alter psychosocial exposures. If they affect millions of workers, even modest individual effects could represent a major public health issue.

01 Changes in working conditions and work organisation02 Adverse psychosocial exposures, if they persist03 Possible effects on mental and physical health

Links between certain psychosocial exposures, depression and cardiovascular disease are documented in the general literature.

The scale of AI's effects remains to be established. This literature does not allow a number of illnesses to be attributed to AI. Effects depend on uses, organisations and preventive measures.

Our thesis

Occupational health must take its place at the heart of AI Safety.

By connecting work as it is actually done, organisation and health, occupational health already has analytical frameworks and preventive measures to detect these changes early. Its role should not begin when workers become unwell: it should begin when tools are chosen and work is redesigned.

A contribution to preventing AI risks, to be coordinated with action by workers, employers, developers and public authorities.

Before choosingUnderstand & anticipateDuring the pilotObserve & adjustOver timeReview & act

Describe the work before choosing the tool.

  • Identify the tasks, responsibilities and teams involved.
  • Establish a baseline and involve workers.
  • Define the expected benefits, risks to monitor and review criteria.

Test the promises against work as it is actually done.

  • Measure checking time, errors and rework.
  • Check that people can correct, challenge or stop the system.
  • Adjust work organisation before extending deployment.

Monitor effects, not just adoption.

  • Compare workload, health and skills with the baseline.
  • Document changes in staffing, income and decision-making power.
  • Distinguish AI's effects from other changes; where possible, compare with teams whose deployment is deferred.
  • Review benefits, address harm and suspend use if necessary.
The six requirements of the proposed framework
  1. Assess risks before deployment.
  2. Give workers an effective voice.
  3. Recognise hidden work.
  4. Ensure meaningful human control.
  5. Preserve skills and social support.
  6. Share benefits and monitor consequences.

A framework proposed in the article by Broutin and Faghri; its own effectiveness remains to be evaluated. It is neither a validated method nor a regulatory standard.

Strengthen prevention

AI can also support those who care for workers' health.

Occupational health professionals can use AI to prepare their work, document a situation and support worker follow-up. These possibilities must be evaluated, just like the uses on which they advise.

The aim: free up useful time for clinical discussion, work analysis and prevention, while preserving professional judgement.

Time savings alone do not demonstrate better follow-up or health. Benefits must be verified; confidentiality, skills and professional responsibility remain central.

The link in the other direction

Protect the work of those who make AI safer.

Occupational health concerns more than AI users. It also concerns the people who build, train and examine the risks of AI systems.

AI laboratories · Evaluation · Red teaming

Who protects the working conditions of safety research?

Fatigue, tight deadlines, value conflicts, insufficient resources or difficulty raising concerns can undermine work that requires attention, expertise and the ability to challenge.

In laboratories developing frontier models, preventing these situations could contribute to the quality of evaluations and safety decisions.

A link to investigate. The reasoning draws on human factors and documented signals. It does not demonstrate that working conditions caused a model safety incident.
