AI safety & AGI.

Models are advancing rapidly, but “general intelligence”, benchmark performance, autonomy and reliability are not the same thing. This page explains what is changing — and why safety must advance at the same pace.

The missing bridge

Model safety is not enough when a system transforms work.

Model-level risks, real deployment conditions and their effects on health are distinct but inseparable levels of analysis. This connection defines the site’s approach to AI safety at work and occupational health and its framework for AI-related psychosocial risks.

01 / Define without oversimplifying

What is artificial general intelligence?

AGI is a research and foresight concept, not a settled technical category. Definitions differ over the human reference level, breadth of tasks and expected degree of autonomy.

An economic definition

Outperform humans in most economically valuable activities.

OpenAI’s Charter defines AGI as highly autonomous systems that outperform humans at most economically valuable work. This definition emphasises broad utility and autonomy.

Read the OpenAI Charter ↗
A classification by levels

Measure breadth of capabilities, performance level and autonomy separately.

Google DeepMind researchers propose classifying systems by the range of tasks they can perform and their performance level, from “emerging” to “superhuman”. Autonomy is treated separately because it substantially changes deployment risk.

Read “Levels of AGI” ↗
01

Foundation model

A model trained on large amounts of data and then adapted to many tasks. It produces outputs; it does not necessarily act on its own.

02

Agent

A system combining a model with tools, memory, an action loop and access rights to pursue a multi-step objective.

03

AGI

The term generally means broad versatility at or above a human level. The precise threshold varies between definitions.

04

Superintelligence

A distinct hypothesis: a system that would greatly exceed the best humans in nearly every relevant intellectual activity.

What AGI does not automatically mean

A good conversation, an excellent mathematics score or successful completion of an autonomous task proves neither general intelligence, consciousness nor human understanding of the world.

02 / Scaling laws

Why more compute often produces better performance.

Scaling laws describe empirical relationships: on certain training metrics, error decreases fairly regularly when model size, data volume and compute increase together.

01
Training computeIncreasing the number of operations generally makes it possible to train larger models or use more data. Energy and hardware costs also increase.
02
Model size and data volumeThe “Chinchilla” work shows that at a fixed compute budget, increasing model size alone is inefficient: data volume must scale with it.
03
Data quality and post-trainingData selection, instruction tuning, human or automated feedback and synthetic data substantially change final behaviour.
04
Inference-time computeReasoning models can spend more compute on a question, explore several paths and use tools. This improves some tasks at the cost of greater latency and expense.

Complex problems

Tools and interfaces

Search, execute code, call software or operate an interface when the application grants these permissions.

The central limitation

These models may produce remarkable work and then fail at a simple step, invent a source or pursue an objective in the wrong way. Fluent output often hides this uncertainty.

05 / Why AI safety is crucial

The more capable a system is, the wider the reach of an error or misuse.

AI safety encompasses work to prevent, detect and reduce harm from AI systems — from ordinary errors to severe risks made possible by highly autonomous models.

01 · Misuse

Useful dual-use capabilities

Coding, scientific research and automation can serve legitimate goals or facilitate an attack. Cyber, biological and chemical risks are subject to specific evaluations at several labs.

Priority: measure dangerous capabilities, limit access and monitor high-risk uses.
02 · Reliability

A wrong but convincing action

A model may misinterpret an instruction, invent information or use a tool incorrectly. In an automated chain, a small error may propagate before it is noticed.

Priority: tests for serious errors, validation, minimal permissions and immediate stopping capability.
03 · Autonomy

More steps, less direct control

When an agent plans, acts and repeats over a long period, predicting every action and identifying when the objective was misunderstood becomes more difficult.

Priority: action boundaries, human validation at irreversible steps and a complete operation log.
04 · Influence

Information, persuasion and decisions

Producing personalised content at scale can amplify disinformation, fraud or manipulation. A well-worded answer can also create excessive confidence in a weak recommendation.

Priority: traceability, transparency, evaluation of influence uses and control over distribution.
05 · Dependency

Concentration and loss of know-how

A small number of models may become infrastructure for many occupations. Outages, version changes, provider dependency or progressive skill loss create systemic risk.

Priority: fallbacks, version control, skill maintenance and reversible choices.
06 · Work

Safety includes occupational health

A system may be technically safe while intensifying workload, reducing autonomy, shifting responsibility or imposing permanent monitoring. These effects must be examined before and after deployment.

Priority: involve workers, observe real work and integrate psychosocial risks into evaluation.
A simple idea

Safety is not about predicting an arrival date for AGI. It is about checking, before every increase in capability or autonomy, that dangerous behaviour can be detected, consequences limited and decisions challenged.

06 / Move from power to control

Five levels of control to put in place before entrusting an action to the system.

Model evaluations are not enough. They must be complemented by technical, organisational and human measures adapted to the real context of use.

01 · Evaluate

Test what could go wrong

Capabilities, serious errors, misuse, robustness, cybersecurity and effects on work.

02 · Limit

Grant minimum permissions

Clearly bound access to data, tools, budget, duration, number of actions and scope.

03 · Authorise

Retain meaningful human validation

An identified owner validates sensitive, irreversible or high-impact decisions.

04 · Monitor

See errors and incidents

Logs, alerts, user feedback, version tracking and an accessible reporting channel.

05 · Correct

Be able to suspend and learn

Stopping procedure, fallback, incident analysis and reassessment after any major change.

07 / Read a model announcement

Four questions to avoid confusing real progress with a commercial demonstration.

These reference points help compare models without reducing quality to one number or repeating provider claims without context.

01Which exact model version was tested, and when?

A product name may cover several versions. Record the precise identifier, date, reasoning mode, permitted tools and settings used.

02Does the score come from the provider or an independent evaluation?

Both can be useful, but an independent, reproducible and documented measure provides greater assurance. Conditions must be identical when comparing models.

03How much time, how many attempts and how much money were required?

Performance obtained with many attempts or a very high compute budget may be unusable in everyday work. Cost, latency and variability are part of the result.

04Which errors remain hidden by the average?

Two models may have the same overall score while making different errors. Examine failures that would have the most serious consequences in the intended use.
