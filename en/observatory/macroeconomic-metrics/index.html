AI at Work Observatory

Macroeconomic metrics.

Who uses AI at work, in which countries, and what do the statistics show about firms, jobs and prevention?

The map compares three international sources, which do not measure the same thing. Other measures follow, each with its population, its date and its source.

By Dr Charles Broutin, occupational physician · 22 November 2026

Three sources, three measures.

To our knowledge, none covers AI use at work in every country. Three come close. Choose one: the map, the ranking and the definition change with it.

The three sources cannot be added up or averaged: they differ in who is counted, in what counts as AI and in their dates. For France, they give 44%, 24.6% and 49.6%.

Worker survey

Trust, attitudes and use of artificial intelligence: A global study 2025, University of Melbourne and KPMG. 48,340 people surveyed online in 47 countries, from November 2024 to mid-January 2025, 32,352 of them in work.

An online panel only reaches people who are online: India is at 91% here and at 18.5% in the usage estimates.

The value for France is read from the report’s ranked chart, between Belgium and Japan, both at 44%. Its country profile prints 59%, which appears to include any use, however rare.

Global report · Country profiles

Official statistics

Individuals – use of generative AI tools (dataset isoc_ai_iaiu), Eurostat, 2025 survey, updated on 5 June 2026. National statistical offices ask people aged 16 to 74 about their use of generative AI in the previous three months.

The map shows use for the job among people in work: employees, the self-employed and family workers. Only generative AI counts, so the measure is narrower than the survey’s. Counting everyone aged 16 to 74, the European Union figure falls from 21.8% to 15.4%.

Eurostat database

Usage estimates

Global AI Diffusion: Q2 2026 Trends and Insights, Microsoft AI Economy Institute, September 2026. Estimated from Microsoft’s aggregated and anonymised usage data, adjusted for market share, internet access and population.

It is the only one of the three sources that covers most of the world, and the only one that is not about work: it counts every use by people aged 15 to 64, in work or not.

36 economies share exactly the same series as their neighbours, for example twelve West African countries at 10.4%, which suggests regional estimates.

Why track the spread of AI.

This site’s thesis: occupational health must take its place at the heart of AI Safety. Safety depends on models and on their interactions with people and organisations. The more AI spreads through work, the more this dimension matters.

A question of scale.

What happens within a team can become a societal issue when the same changes spread and persist. If they affect millions of workers, even modest individual effects could represent a major public health issue.

Two collective risks.

Widespread psychosocial hazards could cause substantial population health harm. Losses of autonomy, skills and bargaining power could signal a broader erosion of human agency, or human disempowerment. These collective risks remain hypotheses to be documented.

Only one axis measured.

The map shows the speed and scale of deployment, the first axis of the AI / prevention ratio. It says nothing about the second: risk assessment, social dialogue, the ability to act and make corrections. Other surveys come closer: see beyond adoption.

Beyond adoption.

Other sources measure what the map leaves out. Four findings, with their orders of magnitude.

Spread and uses

One EU enterprise in five uses AI, up from 13.5% a year earlier. Worldwide, one worker in four is in an occupation exposed to generative AI.

Employment: early signals

At the scale of the economy, the monitoring available (OECD, ECB, Yale) shows no net effect of AI on employment. But hiring of young people is falling more in the most exposed occupations, in France as in the United States, although causality is not established. Perceived effects at work

Nearly one EU worker in two says digital technologies determine the pace of their work. The question covers digital technologies in general, not AI alone.

Read these figures with care.

Announcements, not departures.

The count of layoffs attributed to AI rests on the reason the employer gives. A voluntary exit offer, like Microsoft’s, counts eligible employees, not departures.

Gaps, not causes.

Studies of young people’s employment compare more and less exposed occupations, without establishing that AI is behind the differences observed.

Different scopes.

Each measure has its own population, country and date. The values cannot be added up or compared from one source to another.
