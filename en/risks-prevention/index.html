Psychosocial risks · Artificial intelligence · Prevention

AI becomes a psychosocial risk when it disrupts the organisation of work.

Written by Dr Charles Broutin, occupational physician, AI lead of the French Society of Occupational Health · Updated on August 18, 2026

By redistributing tasks, deadlines, control, responsibility, relationships and skills, an artificial intelligence system (AI) can create new constraints or weaken the resources that make it possible to cope with them. The risk therefore does not come from the model alone, but from the work organized around it.

Direct answer

The essentials

Artificial intelligence becomes a psychosocial risk factor when it sustainably transforms the organisation of work: workload, autonomy, responsibilities, relationships, skills or job security. The available studies do not show a single effect: certain uses improve productivity or control over work, while others are associated with more surveillance, invisible work, loneliness or exhaustion. Prevention must therefore analyse the complete work system, with the people concerned, based on the six dimensions of the Gollac report, then monitor the real effects over time.

  1. 01
    The risk is not in the model alone. It arises from the meeting between a technical capacity, a task, objectives, people and an organisation.
  2. 02
    The results are heterogeneous. The studies cited relate in particular to 5,172 support agents, 197 Australian workers and randomized health trials.
  3. 03
    Prevention remains collective. It describes the work before and afterwards, applies Gollac's six dimensions and makes the project measurable, debatable and reversible.

Central thesis

How does AI become a psychosocial risk at work?

AI can accelerate production, impose a recommendation, monitor activity or automate part of the judgment. If the organisation increases objectives, reduces room for maneuver, makes verification invisible or maintains uncertainty, this transformation can fuel psychosocial risk factors (RPS). To relate this analysis to a concrete situation, consult the course preventing risks linked to AI.

01 / SYSTEM

A capacity is introduced.

Generate, classify, recommend, monitor or contribute to a decision.

02 / ORGANISATION

Work is redistributed.

Tasks, objectives, deadlines, responsibilities, control, cooperation and learning change.

03 / EXHIBITION

Constraints and resources evolve.

Intensity, autonomy, support, conflicts of values, insecurity and invisible work are being recomposed.

04 / HEALTH

Effects may appear.

Depending on the duration, accumulation and margins of action: stress, exhaustion, sleep disorders or cardiovascular damage.

01 / Situate the scale

Epidemiological benchmarks to read without attributing them to AI.

Essential precaution: these orders of magnitude do not allow an “AI disease burden” to be estimated. They remind us why a technology that lastingly modifies workload, autonomy, schedules or insecurity must be studied as a transformation of working conditions.

02 / Results specific to AI

What effects of AI has research already observed?

The studies focus on tools, different professions, durations and indicators. They describe possibilities, not a universal consequence of the use of AI.

In the Generative AI at Work study by Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond, a generative AI assistant increased the number of problems resolved per hour among 5,172 support agents, with particularly large gains among less experienced workers. Consult the study ↗

PRODUCTIVITY

Real gains in certain tasks.

  • Experimental writing tasks were completed faster, with improved average quality.
  • Across 5,172 support agents, one assistant increased the number of issues resolved per hour.
  • Gains were particularly large among less experienced workers.
EFFECTS FAVORABLE

Control and burnout may improve.

  • Among 197 Australian workers, the use of generative AI was associated with more control over work, with no significant association with burnout.
  • In a randomized healthcare trial, ambient AI reduced burnout, interpersonal disengagement, and daily note-writing time.
  • Another trial reduced documentation time for one in two systems; the effects on burnout remain exploratory.
ADVERSE EFFECTS

Other studies report fragilities.

  • Algorithmic management is associated with perceived threat and burnout in a cross-sectional study.
  • Passive copying of generated content reduced self-efficacy, sense of ownership, and sense of purpose in a pre-recorded experiment.
  • Higher interaction with AI was associated with loneliness and adverse after-work outcomes in four studies.

Time saved is not necessarily a health benefit. It becomes one if the organisation reinvests it in recovery, learning, quality, relationships or sustainable staffing. If it is immediately converted into higher targets, it may instead fuel work intensification.

03 / Professional mechanisms

Which professional mechanisms should be monitored?

Three movements make it possible to link scientific results to the tasks, responsibilities and relationships that structure real work.

01 / HUMAN ON THE HOOK

The invisible work of verification.

Production speeds up, but one person remains responsible for verifying the accuracy, legality, and appropriateness of the output. Work shifts to vigilance, integration, exceptions, and error recovery.

Scientific question: Are the time, authority, and skills needed for control actually available?
02 / BEFORE TERMINATION

Insecurity begins with threat.

Credible fear of replacement, perceived loss of skill value, or role uncertainty can produce anticipatory stress even without actual job loss.

Point of vigilance: exposure to uncertainty can long precede a possible reorganisation.
03 / WHAT AI BRINGS AWAY

Autonomy, learning, meaning and connections.

Delegating judgments or exchanges can reduce opportunities to learn, ask for help, build expertise and recognize the quality of one's own work.

Point of caution: a more efficient flow can also become less autonomous, less educational and less social work.

04 / Gollac framework

How does the Gollac framework help analyse AI?

The six dimensions describe the organisation and the job, not the supposed fragility of people. Several dimensions can be modified simultaneously by the same deployment.

G01

Intensity and working time.

Volume, delays, interruptions, schedules and complexity.

With AI: objectives identified, invisible corrections, permanent acceleration.
G02

Emotional demands.

Having to control one's emotions or manage distress and conflicts.

With AI: explain, repair or assume a decision that one can control imperfectly.
G03

Autonomy.

Choose the method, the pace and mobilize your skills.

With AI: recommendation difficult to reject, monitoring and imposed workflow.
G04

Social relations.

Support, cooperation, recognition and quality of management.

With AI: replaced exchanges, reduced mentoring, responsibilities without interlocutor.
G05

Conflicts of values.

Be able to produce useful work that complies with the rules of the profession.

With AI: speed favored over quality or output contrary to professional judgment.
G06

Job insecurity.

Uncertainty about job, role, income and future changes.

With AI: unclear future tasks, continuous reorganisation and devalued skills.

05 / State of the evidence

Prevent without claiming that everything has already been demonstrated.

The literature on the mental effects of precise deployments remains limited, fragmented and heterogeneous. Causal inferences are constrained by the methods, contexts and indicators used.

ESTABLISHED

Working conditions matter for health.

  • High demands associated with low control are linked to clinical depression and coronary heart disease.
  • Insecurity, organisational injustice, and low support have documented health associations.
  • Prevention should target organisational factors, not just individual adaptation.
EMERGING

AI redistributes multiple exposures.

  • Verification, monitoring and accountability can create additional work.
  • The gains, risks and populations concerned vary greatly depending on the work system.
  • Favorable and unfavorable effects can coexist in the same organisation.
UNKNOWN

The scale of health effects specifically attributable to AI.

  • There is no data to extrapolate a global disease burden attributable to AI at work.
  • Long-term effects and causal relationships require longitudinal studies and intervention trials.
  • Independent access to deployment data remains necessary.

Limited evidence is neither evidence of inevitable harm nor evidence that no risk exists. It justifies proportionate prevention, long-term observation and the possibility of reviewing the work system.
