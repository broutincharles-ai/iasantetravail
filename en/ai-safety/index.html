AI safety · Reference page

AI safety, seen from the workplace.

AI safety brings together the research and practices that aim to prevent harm caused by artificial intelligence systems. This page sets out the essentials and shows what occupational health can contribute.

By Dr Charles Broutin, occupational physician · Updated on 16 November 2026

Read the glossary →The JOEM editorial →

The essentials

Preventing harm, not just errors.

What the term covers, in three sentences.

Direct answer

AI safety refers to the research and practices that aim to prevent and reduce the harm AI systems can cause to people and society, from design to use.

For general-purpose AI, the International AI Safety Report (February 2026) distinguishes malicious use, malfunctions and systemic risks, which include effects on employment and on human autonomy.

Work is one of the first places where these risks become concrete. Occupational health knows how to observe and prevent these effects: that is the thesis of this site, and its scope remains to be documented.

International AI Safety Report 2026 ↗

01 / Definition

A technical field, and a sociotechnical one.

Three ways into the subject.

The systems

Making a model do what is expected of it.

Part of the research focuses on the models themselves: aligning them with the intentions of those who build and use them, understanding how they work internally, and testing their capabilities and behaviour before release.

AlignmentEvaluations
The uses

Assessing a system in its real context.

The same model does not have the same effects depending on the task, the people and the organisation. A sociotechnical approach adds the study of human interaction and societal effects to capability testing.

Weidinger et al., 2023 ↗
The rules

Governing development and deployment.

Laws, codes of practice, public evaluation institutes and company commitments set out who must assess what, against which thresholds, and what must be reported.

AI at work: the law
Safety, security, and the French words

French translates AI safety as either “sécurité” or “sûreté”. In occupational safety and health, “sécurité” already means preventing accidents and harm to health. Protecting AI systems against attacks is a separate matter: AI security. See the glossary

02 / Risks

Three families of risk.

The classification used by the 2026 international report, and what it shows at work.

FamilyWhat it coversAt work, for example
Malicious useAI-generated content used to defraud or deceive, influence and manipulation, cyberattacks, biological and chemical risks.More convincing fraud targeting employees (fake executives, synthetic voice or video), fabricated content used for harassment.
MalfunctionsUnreliability, unexpected behaviour and loss-of-control scenarios. The report considers that current systems lack the capabilities to escape all control, but that they are becoming more autonomous.Plausible errors to catch, uncounted checking time, tools that tell users what they want to hear, agents acting on messages or files without sufficient oversight.
Systemic risksEffects of large-scale diffusion on the labour market and on human autonomy. The report notes signs of declining demand for early-career workers in some AI-exposed occupations.Fewer entry-level roles, dependence on tools, deskilling, work intensification, weaker bargaining power.

The report makes no policy recommendations. It describes an “evidence dilemma”: acting too early risks ineffective measures, while waiting for solid evidence may leave society exposed. Read the report ↗

03 / Methods

Assess, govern, monitor, correct.

The tools of AI safety, and their counterparts in prevention.

  1. Assess before release.

    Capability and behaviour testing, and red-teaming exercises in which teams try to make the system fail. In prevention, this is the risk assessment carried out before an organisational change: describe the work, anticipate, involve the people concerned. Assess an AI project

  2. Set thresholds and stick to them.

    Developers of the most advanced models publish safety frameworks that define risk thresholds and the measures planned if they are reached. In Europe, the AI Act’s Code of Practice sets out what is expected of models with systemic risk. Safety frameworks

  3. Keep humans able to oversee.

    The AI Act requires high-risk systems to be designed so that people can oversee, understand, override and stop them. In real work, oversight takes time, skills and the power to say no. Human in the loop

  4. Monitor after deployment.

    Collect incidents and weak signals once systems are in use. Occupational health already does this for work-related disease. AI incidents

  5. Be able to correct, suspend and warn.

    A safety arrangement only works if a use can be rolled back or suspended, and if those who raise the alarm are protected. Whistleblowing

04 / Work

Three links between work and AI safety.

The thesis of this site, with its limits.

Where risks materialise

Work, where AI is deployed.

AI systems allocate tasks, assess, monitor or decide in place of people. The AI Act classifies some of these uses in employment and worker management as high-risk.

AI and psychosocial risksWhen AI becomes the manager
Where they become visible

Occupational health as an early-warning system.

The effects of a deployment often show up first in work: workload, autonomy, skills, entry routes for beginners. Spotting them and reporting them at collective level is part of what occupational health is for.

The economic and social risk
Where they are checked

The work of those who make AI safer.

Evaluators, red teamers, annotators: the quality of safety checks also depends on their working conditions and their ability to raise concerns.

The preprintThe commentary
What this thesis does not claim

It does not claim that occupational health sums up AI safety, nor that working conditions have caused a model safety incident. Its contribution remains to be documented and evaluated; the six-requirement framework proposed in Broutin and Faghri’s editorial (JOEM, 2026) is neither a validated method nor a regulatory standard.

Key answers

Four frequent questions.

Short answers, detailed in the glossary.

AI safety or AI security: what is the difference?

AI safety is about preventing harm from AI systems, whether through failures, misuse or systemic effects. AI security protects the systems themselves against attacks such as data poisoning or model theft. In French, “sécurité” is the word used by INESIA and CeSIA for AI safety; “sûreté” stresses the prevention of failures, as in nuclear safety.

Is AI safety only about the most powerful models?

No. Part of the work focuses on frontier models and their dangerous capabilities, but the field also covers ordinary failures, malicious use and the effects of AI diffusion, including on employment and autonomy.

Why would occupational health be concerned?

Because work is one of the first places where AI is deployed at scale, and occupational health already has methods for observing the health effects of change and preventing them. This contribution remains to be documented and evaluated.

What is gradual disempowerment?

It is the hypothesis, put forward by Kulveit and colleagues in 2025, of a progressive loss of human influence over the economy, culture and institutions as AI replaces human labour and cognition, without any abrupt takeover.
