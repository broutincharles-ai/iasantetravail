The French CSE and AI at work: assess a project before issuing its opinion

A practical framework to help the French Social and Economic Committee (CSE) obtain the information it needs, assess how AI may affect work and identify missing safeguards.

By Dr Charles Broutin, occupational physician · Updated on 13 November 2026

In brief

As of 13 November 2026

Who is concerned
In companies with at least 50 employees, the employer informs and consults the CSE before introducing a new technology or a significant change to working conditions (Article L. 2312-8 of the Labour Code).
When
Before the decision. Absent an agreement, the committee has one month to give its opinion, two months if it calls in an expert (Article R. 2312-6). Deadlines, experts, remedies

Why it matters

Why should the CSE engage with AI at work?

Direct answer

Introducing AI changes more than the tools people use. It can redistribute decision-making, objectives, review work, autonomy, skills, oversight and accountability. The CSE provides a forum in which these changes can be examined collectively before they become embedded in the organisation.

01

Intervene when the project can still evolve.

When consultation is required, it precedes the decision. The CSE can request information and safeguards while the choices remain open.

02

Examine actual capabilities.

An assistant can also access emails, files, data or software. The risk depends on the entire system, not just the intended use.

03

Make additional work visible.

Checking, correcting, handling exceptions and recovering from errors takes time. This workload must be measured and recognised.

04

Organise collective monitoring.

The system may evolve after launch. Incident handling, stopping thresholds and changes that trigger reassessment must be specified.

2025–2026 case law

Courts have found that CSE consultation was required before generative AI tools were introduced, including in a case where their use was presented as optional. On 21 May 2026, the Paris Court of Appeal upheld the suspension of ChatGPT and an editorial assistant rolled out without consultation.

Occupational health

Can AI at work cause psychosocial risks?

Direct answer

Yes, depending on how it is used and organised. AI can contribute to work intensification, reduce scope for discretion, increase monitoring, undermine meaning at work or shift accountability. These risks must be assessed through the realities of the work itself.

Points to examine with employees
  • Workload, pace and interruptions
  • Autonomy and scope for discretion
  • Skills, quality and meaning of work
  • Monitoring and evaluation of activity
  • Teams and working relationships
  • Occupational change and job security
Expert input available to the CSE

Seek advice from the occupational physician.

The occupational physician advises employee representatives and can help the CSE assess the project’s possible effects on health and working conditions. The physician attends relevant CSE meetings in an advisory capacity and may draw on the multidisciplinary team of the occupational health and prevention service (SPST).

Article R.4623-1 ↗Article L.2314-3 ↗
Safety obligation

The employer must protect physical and mental health.

This duty applies to an AI project as to any reorganisation. On 15 September 2026, the Créteil Judicial Court suspended a reorganisation handing about 70% of eight sub-editors’ tasks to AI: its effects on workload and health had not been sufficiently assessed.

Article L. 4121-1 ↗The decision →
Risk assessment document

Have the project’s risks recorded in the DUERP.

The CSE contributes to risk assessment and is consulted on the single risk assessment document (DUERP) and its updates. An update is required for any decision involving a significant change to health and safety conditions or working conditions. In companies with at least 50 employees, the assessment leads to an annual prevention programme.

Article L. 4121-3 ↗Article R. 4121-2 ↗AI and the DUERP (in French) →
CSSCT

Prepare the consultation in committee.

Where it exists, the health, safety and working conditions committee (CSSCT) may prepare the review of the project by delegation. Calling in an expert and giving the opinion remain with the CSE.

Article L. 2315-38 ↗
Occupational physician

Ask for written input before the opinion.

When the occupational physician identifies a risk to workers’ health, they propose measures in writing to preserve it. Their proposals and the employer’s reply are sent to the CSE.

Article L. 4624-9 ↗Template letter (in French) →

Consultation · How it works

Deadlines, experts, remedies: what the Labour Code provides.

Four reference points for timing the consultation and knowing what to do when it does not take place.

Deadlines

One month, two with an expert.

Absent an agreement, the CSE is deemed to have given a negative opinion after one month. This becomes two months when an expert is involved, and three months when expert assessments take place in a consultation conducted both at central CSE level and in one or more establishment CSEs.

Article R. 2312-6 ↗
Information

Precise, written information.

The committee must have sufficient time for review and precise, written information (Article L. 2312-15). If it considers the information insufficient, it may apply to the president of the judicial court under the accelerated procedure on the merits.

The 10 questions to ask ↓
Expert

A certified expert, 80% paid by the employer.

The CSE may call in a certified expert when new technologies are introduced or a major project changes health and safety conditions or working conditions. The cost is then shared: 80% for the employer, 20% from the committee’s operating budget (Article L. 2315-80).

Article L. 2315-94 ↗Ministry of Labour fact sheet (in French) ↗
Remedies

The summary judge can suspend the project.

When the employer implements the project without consultation, the CSE may apply to the summary judge, who can order the consultation and suspend the rollout until it is completed. Obstructing the committee’s regular functioning is also punishable by a fine of €7,500.

Article L. 2317-1 ↗

01 / Minimum evidence base

Ten questions to ask before the CSE issues its opinion.

For each question, indicate whether the employer’s response is documented, incomplete or missing. The summary updates automatically.

01Purpose

What problem are we trying to solve with AI?

A clearly defined need, the alternatives considered and the expected benefit.

02Capabilities

What can the system actually do?

Its available functions — not only the stated use case.

03Decision

What decisions can it make or influence?

Distinguish between assistance, recommendation, scoring and decision-making.

04Autonomy

What actions can it perform without human intervention?

Identify the actions performed, their scope and frequency.

05Access

What data and tools does it have access to?

Employee data, emails, files, internet access and workplace software.

06Scope

Which employees and occupations will be affected?

Groups exposed, tasks transformed and situations requiring particular attention.

07Actual work

What effects are expected on work?

Workload, pace, autonomy, skills, teamwork, monitoring and employment.

08Recourse

How can a human challenge, correct or stop the system?

An identified person with the necessary time, information and authority.

09Incidents

How will unexpected effects be detected?

A reporting channel, logs, a named owner and a feedback process.

10Suspension

Under what circumstances will the system be suspended?

Written shutdown thresholds and a tested rollback procedure.

02 / Authority given to the system

Assess the system’s actual autonomy.

The higher the level, the stronger the justification, oversight, monitoring and reversibility must be.

Human control test

Who can stop the AI? Does this person actually have the time, information, skills and authority to do so?

Work and health indicator

Is the project likely to change the organisation, workload, autonomy, pace or working conditions?

Recommendation

This project may affect working conditions. Seek advice from the occupational physician or the SPST multidisciplinary team before the CSE issues its opinion.

03 / The full assessment framework

Seven review stages, in decision-making order.

A concise pathway that starts with the system, examines the work as it is actually done and ends with a reasoned conclusion.

01

Understand the system

Model, vendor, version, hosting, memory, internet, tools and agents.

02

See the transformed work

Workload, autonomy, skills, working relationships, assessment and employment.

03

Assess autonomy

From A0 information to A4 organisational delegation.

04

Demonstrate effective human oversight

Time, information, skills, authority and real possibility of stopping.

05

Prepare for incidents

Reporting, severity, analysis, suspension and rollback.

06

Organise reassessment

New model, new data, new tools or a new population.

07

Formulate a conclusion

What is documented, what is missing and which safeguards need strengthening.

04 / Essential answers

Frequently asked questions about the CSE and AI.

Clear answers about consultation, occupational health and the information the CSE should request.

Should the CSE be consulted before any use of AI?

An employee’s occasional, individual use of an AI tool does not in itself trigger CSE consultation. In undertakings with at least 50 employees, however, an employer-led introduction of AI may fall within the CSE’s prior information and consultation remit, notably where new technologies are introduced or the organisation and working conditions are affected. Recent decisions have required consultation before generative AI tools were introduced, including in a case where their use was presented as optional. Conversely, a limited, voluntary trial with no identified effect on work was held insufficient to require it (Paris Judicial Court, 10 February 2026).

Specific rules

Certain uses are also subject to specific rules. In particular, the CSE must be informed in advance about recruitment-assistance methods and automated personnel-management processes, and must be informed and consulted before means of monitoring employee activity are implemented. Article L.2312-38 of the Labour Code ↗

Can the CSE seek advice from the occupational physician during an AI project?

Yes. The occupational physician advises employee representatives on health and working conditions. When AI may change the organisation, workload, autonomy or pace of work, or expose employees to psychosocial risks, the CSE may seek the physician’s advice. The occupational physician also attends relevant CSE meetings in an advisory capacity.

Why should the CSE engage with AI projects at work?

Because an AI system can redistribute decisions, review work, autonomy, skills, monitoring and accountability. The CSE enables these changes to be examined collectively before they become embedded in everyday working arrangements.

What information should the CSE request about an AI system?

At a minimum: its purpose, actual capabilities, the decisions it influences, its level of autonomy, accessible data and tools, the employees affected, expected effects on work, human oversight, incident monitoring and suspension criteria.

Can an optional AI tool still fall within the CSE’s remit?

Yes, depending on the circumstances. Describing a tool as optional does not rule out effects on working methods, workload, organisation or skills. The project must be assessed by reference to its capabilities, scope and actual effects.

How long does the CSE have to give its opinion on an AI project?

Absent an agreement setting other time limits, the CSE is deemed to have given a negative opinion after one month. This becomes two months when an expert is involved, and three months when expert assessments take place in a consultation conducted both at central CSE level and in one or more establishment CSEs. Article R. 2312-6 of the Labour Code ↗

Can the CSE call in an expert on an AI project, and who pays?

Yes. The CSE may call in a certified expert when new technologies are introduced or a major project changes health and safety conditions or working conditions. The cost is then borne 80% by the employer and 20% by the committee, from its operating budget (Article L. 2315-80). Article L. 2315-94 of the Labour Code ↗

What does an employer risk by rolling out AI without consulting the CSE?

The CSE may apply to the summary judge. Since February 2025, several judges have ordered the consultation and suspended the tool until it was completed, sometimes with a daily penalty. Obstructing the committee’s regular functioning is also punishable by a fine of €7,500 (Article L. 2317-1). Not every situation leads there: in February 2026, the Paris Judicial Court held that a four-month voluntary trial did not yet require consultation. See the decisions

Must the risk assessment document (DUERP) be updated when AI is introduced?

Not automatically. The document must be updated for any decision involving a significant change to health and safety conditions or working conditions, or when new information about a risk comes to the employer’s attention; it is updated at least once a year in companies with at least eleven employees. The CSE is consulted on the document and its updates. Article R. 4121-2 of the Labour Code ↗

A preparation tool to use alongside the applicable legal framework.

Not every use of AI automatically triggers consultation. The workforce, the nature of the project, its capabilities and its effects must be assessed on a case-by-case basis.
