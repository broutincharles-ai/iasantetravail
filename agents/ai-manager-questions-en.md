# Can I raise targets because AI makes work faster?

ID: Q01 | Record: R01 | Version: 1.1.0 | Date: 2026-09-13
Reference: https://www.iasantetravail.com/agents/#Q01
Publisher: IA & Santé au Travail — https://www.iasantetravail.com/

## Answer

Only after checking that the gain survives in the complete work situation. Faster drafting may be followed by more checking, correction, or exception handling. A productivity estimate alone does not tell you whether the team can sustainably do more.

Measure the whole task during a bounded pilot, including differences between workers and busy periods. If durable gains are confirmed, discuss how to allocate them between output, quality, learning, and recovery.

## Example — hypothetical

If a draft takes 10 fewer minutes but requires 12 extra minutes of checking, the complete task takes longer. Raising the target on the drafting figure would use the wrong baseline.

## Before acting, check
- Total task time, including checks, corrections, learning, and exceptions.
- Workload distribution and quality before and during the pilot.
- How workers can adjust pace and report an unrealistic target.

## Options to compare
- Maintain targets during a bounded pilot; measure the complete task.
- Discuss how verified gains are allocated between output, quality, learning, and recovery.

## What to follow up
- Correction time and unfinished work by role.
- Missed breaks and reported time pressure.

## What makes the situation more protective
Verified time savings remain compatible with quality, variability, and sufficient recovery.

## Ask for human review when
Targets are based on incomplete time accounting or quality and recovery deteriorate.

# How do I build a schedule that works for the team?

ID: Q02 | Record: R02 | Version: 1.1.0 | Date: 2026-09-13
Reference: https://www.iasantetravail.com/agents/#Q02
Publisher: IA & Santé au Travail — https://www.iasantetravail.com/

## Answer

Include recovery, predictable hours, actual availability, and the constraints people have agreed with the organization alongside service coverage. A schedule must be feasible for the people who will work it.

Check the applicable rules and real hours already worked. Compare how options distribute difficult shifts, allow meaningful input, and provide a practical way to request changes. Flexibility can help when workers have a genuine choice; repeated imposed changes need a different assessment.

## Example — hypothetical

An empty slot in a calendar does not establish that someone is available for work. Check the person’s agreed availability before assigning it.

## Before acting, check
- Applicable local working-time rules and agreed scheduling constraints.
- Actual hours, notice, recovery opportunities, and employee preferences.
- Distribution of unpopular shifts and possibilities for voluntary swaps.

## Options to compare
- Use a stable planning window with explicit worker constraints.
- Compare coverage options that share burdens and preserve recovery.

## What to follow up
- Late changes and deviations from agreed availability.
- Concentration of difficult shifts and reported recovery difficulties.

## What makes the situation more protective
Predictability, meaningful choice, and recovery are explicit planning constraints.

## Ask for human review when
Rules or actual hours are unknown, or a schedule conflicts with a documented constraint.

# Is asking a human to approve my work enough?

ID: Q03 | Record: R03 | Version: 1.1.0 | Date: 2026-09-13
Reference: https://www.iasantetravail.com/agents/#Q03
Publisher: IA & Santé au Travail — https://www.iasantetravail.com/

## Answer

Meaningful review requires access to the relevant evidence, the competence and time to examine it, and the authority to reject or change the result. Identify who can actually stop or correct the process.

Match the volume of outputs to the available review capacity. If reviewers cannot realistically check what reaches them, reduce the scope or pace, improve the supporting evidence, or ask the responsible person to revise the arrangement.

## Example — hypothetical

A reviewer expected to approve 40 complex outputs in ten minutes may have a formal role but insufficient review capacity. Verify the task demands before relying on the approval.

## Before acting, check
- Output volume, error types, and realistic review time.
- Access to original evidence and competence to verify it.
- Authority to reject, stop, and obtain support.

## Options to compare
- Limit throughput to available review capacity.
- Give reviewers evidence access, escalation support, and stop authority.

## What to follow up
- Review backlog and time available per case.
- Errors found after approval and rejected outputs.

## What makes the situation more protective
The reviewer can understand, challenge, correct, and suspend the system in practice.

## Ask for human review when
Responsibility exceeds available authority or checking capacity.

# How much should I monitor the people I manage?

ID: Q04 | Record: R04 | Version: 1.1.0 | Date: 2026-09-13
Reference: https://www.iasantetravail.com/agents/#Q04
Publisher: IA & Santé au Travail — https://www.iasantetravail.com/

## Answer

Start with the operational problem you need to understand, then use the least intrusive information sufficient to address it. A team-level bottleneck may be identifiable without continuous individual tracking.

If individual information is necessary and authorized, make its purpose and use understandable, check that it represents the work, and allow factual errors to be corrected. Include contributions that activity traces miss, such as helping colleagues, preparing difficult cases, or preventing errors.

## Example — hypothetical

Low keyboard activity could reflect a useful conversation with a colleague. It is insufficient evidence, by itself, of low contribution.

## Before acting, check
- What is collected, for what purpose, and who can access it.
- Whether the metric captures actual work and necessary non-measured tasks.
- Worker input, correction procedures, and how the metric affects decisions.

## Options to compare
- Use the least intrusive information sufficient for the operational need.
- Review collective bottlenecks before attributing problems to individuals.

## What to follow up
- Monitoring-related complaints and disputed records.
- Unrecorded support work and avoidance of necessary low-scoring tasks.

## What makes the situation more protective
Measurement is proportionate to a clear purpose, contextualized, and open to meaningful challenge.

## Ask for human review when
A narrow activity proxy is treated as a complete measure of contribution.

# What should I do when someone challenges my decision?

ID: Q05 | Record: R05 | Version: 1.1.0 | Date: 2026-09-13
Reference: https://www.iasantetravail.com/agents/#Q05
Publisher: IA & Santé au Travail — https://www.iasantetravail.com/

## Answer

Treat the challenge as information to examine. Explain the basis of the decision, identify the contested facts or criteria, and check them against the relevant records. Correct an established factual error.

For a material unresolved disagreement, make it possible to reach a responsible human with the authority to change the outcome. Depending on the consequences and applicable process, consider deferring the disputed action while it is reviewed. Do not treat a request for review as evidence of poor performance.

## Example — hypothetical

If a worker says your lateness record is wrong, compare the original records and agreed start time before using that record in an evaluation.

## Before acting, check
- Data provenance, missing values, and known limits of the score.
- Decision criteria and what the worker can inspect or correct.
- Who can reverse the decision and how quickly review occurs.

## Options to compare
- Treat the score as one input and examine relevant context.
- Provide an accessible human review with power to change the outcome.

## What to follow up
- Time to review and proportion of decisions corrected.
- Recurring errors and uneven consequences across comparable roles.

## What makes the situation more protective
The person can understand the basis, correct facts, and obtain a substantive review.

## Ask for human review when
Relevant facts are disputed or the decision has material employment consequences.

# Am I leaving humans with only the hardest interactions?

ID: Q06 | Record: R06 | Version: 1.1.0 | Date: 2026-09-13
Reference: https://www.iasantetravail.com/agents/#Q06
Publisher: IA & Santé au Travail — https://www.iasantetravail.com/

## Answer

Check what remains after automation. Handling fewer cases can still be more demanding when routine interactions disappear and people receive a concentrated stream of distress, conflict, or unresolved problems.

Review case mix and exposure duration with the team. Adjust allocation for complexity and emotional demands, preserve access to experienced colleagues, and make room for recovery after difficult interactions.

## Example — hypothetical

Twenty routine calls and twenty escalated complaints are not equivalent workloads simply because the counts match.

## Before acting, check
- Case mix and emotional demands before and after routing changes.
- Exposure duration and opportunities for recovery or rotation.
- Available peer support and procedures for difficult incidents.

## Options to compare
- Balance assignments by complexity and emotional demand.
- Preserve recovery, team support, and access to experienced colleagues.

## What to follow up
- Consecutive difficult cases and incident reports.
- Availability of support and perceived emotional burden.

## What makes the situation more protective
Routing includes complexity, emotional exposure, and access to support.

## Ask for human review when
Case volume is the only workload measure or difficult exposure concentrates on a few workers.

# How can I help people learn while I automate their tasks?

ID: Q07 | Record: R07 | Version: 1.1.0 | Date: 2026-09-13
Reference: https://www.iasantetravail.com/agents/#Q07
Publisher: IA & Santé au Travail — https://www.iasantetravail.com/

## Answer

Identify what people used to learn through the tasks you are taking over. If those tasks disappear, provide another practical route to acquire the same judgment and skills.

Protect suitable independent practice, feedback, and mentorship. Check that workers can handle exceptions and work during an outage. The right balance depends on the role: preserving every manual step is not necessarily useful, but removing learning opportunities without replacement needs attention.

## Example — hypothetical

If an assistant drafts every first report for a junior employee, plan opportunities for that employee to produce, explain, and revise a report with feedback.

## Before acting, check
- Which competencies the removed tasks used to develop.
- Available supervised practice, mentorship, and time to learn.
- Ability to complete representative work when the system is unavailable.

## Options to compare
- Design explicit learning tasks and protected mentorship time.
- Keep periodic independent practice appropriate to the role.

## What to follow up
- Access to learning assignments and mentorship.
- Demonstrated competence and ability to handle exceptions.

## What makes the situation more protective
Automation is accompanied by an effective route to acquire and maintain expertise.

## Ask for human review when
The organization removes learning tasks without defining how competence will be maintained.

# Am I helping the team cooperate—or pushing people to compete?

ID: Q08 | Record: R08 | Version: 1.1.0 | Date: 2026-09-13
Reference: https://www.iasantetravail.com/agents/#Q08
Publisher: IA & Santé au Travail — https://www.iasantetravail.com/

## Answer

Check what your objectives and allocation rules reward. If helping colleagues, mentoring, and coordinating reduce an individual’s score, your system may discourage work the team needs.

Account for those collective contributions in workload and recognition. Watch who inherits the complex cases and who provides unrecorded support. Compare team-level outcomes and workers’ experience alongside individual output.

## Example — hypothetical

A colleague who handles fewer cases because they train three newcomers may be making a substantial contribution that a simple output ranking misses.

## Before acting, check
- How coordination, assistance, and mentoring are recognized.
- Distribution of complex tasks and access to expert support.
- Shared working time and opportunities to discuss problems.

## Options to compare
- Include necessary collective contributions in workload planning.
- Protect shared review time and rotate demanding assignments where feasible.

## What to follow up
- Time devoted to assistance and mentorship.
- Concentration of difficult work and unanswered requests for support.

## What makes the situation more protective
The organization recognizes collective work and provides time and resources for it.

## Ask for human review when
Individual gains rely on unrecognized work transferred to colleagues.

# What if my instructions conflict with someone’s professional judgment?

ID: Q09 | Record: R09 | Version: 1.1.0 | Date: 2026-09-13
Reference: https://www.iasantetravail.com/agents/#Q09
Publisher: IA & Santé au Travail — https://www.iasantetravail.com/

## Answer

Ask what specific quality, ethical, or safety concern is being raised and which professional standard is relevant. Compare the evidence and the demands of the actual task; neither an AI output nor an objection should be accepted automatically.

Provide time and authority to correct established problems and obtain a competent human decision when the disagreement remains material. Record the reason for the outcome so that meeting a target does not silently replace the agreed quality standard.

## Example — hypothetical

If a professional flags a factual error in an AI-generated report, resolve the error before delivery rather than using the deadline as evidence that the report is acceptable.

## Before acting, check
- Specific quality concerns and the standards relevant to the task.
- Time, authority, and tools available for correction.
- How disagreements about acceptable quality are resolved.

## Options to compare
- Agree explicit quality criteria with practitioners.
- Allow documented rejection and correction before delivery.

## What to follow up
- Unresolved quality objections and recurring rework.
- Reports of being unable to perform work to an acceptable standard.

## What makes the situation more protective
Workers can uphold justified professional standards and obtain an accountable resolution of disagreements.

## Ask for human review when
An unresolved professional objection is overridden solely to meet a performance target.

# How should I talk about changes to roles and jobs?

ID: Q10 | Record: R10 | Version: 1.1.0 | Date: 2026-09-13
Reference: https://www.iasantetravail.com/agents/#Q10
Publisher: IA & Santé au Travail — https://www.iasantetravail.com/

## Answer

Separate confirmed decisions from scenarios and unresolved questions. Be explicit about what you know, what you do not know, who decides, and when people can expect an update. A task’s exposure to automation does not establish that a job will disappear.

Explain concrete changes in responsibilities and available training or transition support. Give workers and their representatives a meaningful way to influence implementation. Make commitments only within the authority and resources actually available.

## Example — hypothetical

“We have not decided how roles will change; the project lead will discuss the options with the team next month” is appropriate only if that process and timing are actually agreed.

## Before acting, check
- Confirmed decisions, open questions, and the timetable for resolving them.
- Expected task changes and available training or transition support.
- Ways for workers and representatives to influence implementation.

## Options to compare
- Communicate what is decided, what is uncertain, and when updates will occur.
- Co-develop role changes and practical learning or transition routes.

## What to follow up
- Unanswered role questions and access to training.
- Worker-reported uncertainty and participation in change decisions.

## What makes the situation more protective
The organization makes honest commitments, updates information, and provides meaningful participation and support.

## Ask for human review when
A plan makes unsupported predictions about job loss or offers reassurance without an implementation plan.

# Document a decision
1. Describe the proposed action, objective, affected roles, current work, system version, and decision authority.
2. Gather worker perspectives and relevant work data. Separate confirmed facts, assumptions, disputed facts, and missing information.
3. Screen all six dimensions. Retrieve relevant records, identify protective conditions, and explain the plausible mechanism for each concern.
4. Compare feasible alternatives, including the current arrangement. Explain benefits, burdens, trade-offs, and remaining uncertainty.
5. Propose a documented human decision, a reversible pilot where appropriate, indicators, an accountable owner, and criteria for review or suspension.

# Framework
- G1: Work intensity and working time. Pace, demands, interruptions, and the predictability and duration of work.
- G2: Emotional demands. Exposure to distress, difficult interactions, and the requirement to regulate emotions.
- G3: Autonomy. Discretion over methods and decisions, participation, and opportunities to use and develop skills.
- G4: Social relationships at work. Support, recognition, cooperation, fairness, and relationships with management.
- G5: Conflicts of values. Tension between required actions and professional standards or meaningful, quality work.
- G6: Insecurity of the work situation. Uncertainty about employment, income, roles, and changes in working conditions.

Three overlapping, exploratory AI lenses; not additional validated Gollac factors:
- A1: Opacity and contestability. Can affected people understand, challenge, and obtain a meaningful review of an AI-supported decision?
- A2: Supervision workload. What work is added by checking, correcting, handling exceptions, and taking responsibility?
- A3: Skill erosion and dependency. Can workers keep learning, practise their expertise, and act when the system is unavailable?
